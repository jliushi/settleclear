Amazon reserve explained: why funds are held

A reserve is money Amazon holds back from your balance to cover things that might go wrong later — refunds, A-to-z guarantee claims, and chargebacks. It is normal, it is not a penalty, and it is not lost. This page explains how the reserve works, the two reserve lines you will see in a settlement, and why your bank deposit can be smaller than the settlement total. It pairs with deferred transactions and why your payout is less than your sales.

What the reserve is for

When you sell, Amazon may need to refund a buyer or resolve a claim after the fact. To make sure the money is there, it holds a reserve against your account balance. The standard timing follows a delivery-date policy: funds generally become available around 7 to 14 days after delivery. The reserve is held from your total account balance — importantly, it is not deducted from any single order. There are no transaction-level reserves, even when a reserve results from a specific chargeback.

The two reserve lines in your settlement

Each settlement report shows the reserve as two entries, which is what confuses people:

LineWhat it is
Previous Reserve Amount BalanceA credit — the reserve held last cycle, now released to you.
Current Reserve AmountA debit — the reserve being held from this cycle.

The net of the two is how much reserve moved this period. In a steady business the amount held and the amount released are similar, so the net effect is small; during a sales ramp, more is held than released, and your deposit dips relative to your activity.

Why the deposit is under the settlement total

The settlement report's total-amount is what Amazon owes you for the cycle. The amount that actually lands in your bank can be lower because a reserve was held. That is the gap. When you reconcile, tie your line items to total-amount first, then treat the reserve as a separate timing movement — money that shifts between cycles, not a cost.

How to account for it

In bookkeeping, a reserve is a balance-sheet item, not a profit-and-loss expense. It is your money, temporarily held, scheduled to release. Recording it as an expense understates both your assets and your profit.
